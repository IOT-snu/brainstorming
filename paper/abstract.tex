An intrusion detector that runs on an IoT device must keep learning, because normal traffic drifts. But
a detector that labels its own traffic can be walked toward an attack by a slow poisoner, and a defence
that stops learning when traffic looks suspicious can be frozen by an attacker who trips it and leaves.
From a single device's stream, honest drift and slow poisoning cannot be told apart. We observe that IoT
devices rarely live alone: most honest drift is shared by a device's neighbours, while a poisoning
campaign reaches only the devices its exploit reaches. Fleet-Orthogonal Adaptation (FOA) gives each
device a robust consensus of vulnerability-diverse peers, exchanged as 92-byte authenticated ESP-NOW
frames: the part of a device's update its peers back is applied as far as the device's own traffic agrees,
and the device-specific rest passes through a budget that never closes. We prove that an attacker on one device gains at most
$B_0+Bt$ alarm radii of movement, that fewer than half key-holding Byzantine peers add a bounded bias per
cycle, that FOA has no gate an attacker can trip to stop it learning, and that device-only drift and poisoning are indistinguishable for any rule.
On all nine N-BaIoT devices with real Mirai and BASHLITE traffic, under strong injected shared drift,
FOA cuts attack-attributable compromise from \AutoComp\ (single-device adaptation) to \FOAComp; it is never
compromised by the classic boundary poisoner, resists a monoculture botnet that defeats same-model
consensus, and is unaffected by a freeze attack that locks an alarm-rate switch-off at
\KLFrozenFPR\ false alarms. On 40 weeks of real CESNET traffic it is never compromised, although
there a static detector raises fewer false alarms than any adaptive rule. Three ESP8266 boards showed
two failures that simulation missed: identical boards shrank their detectors together until false
alarms reached 90\%, which a coverage floor removes in simulation and on the boards, and an abrupt
doubling of the traffic rate left no accepted traffic to learn from. The floor also revises our claim:
with it a single device raises fewer false alarms than FOA (FOA's earlier advantage, \FOAFleetFPR\
against \AutoFleetFPR, came from the same shrinking), but is compromised in \FlAutoFCompStrong\ of
attacked runs under drift against FOA's \FlFOAFCompStrong. No attack has yet been run on the boards.
